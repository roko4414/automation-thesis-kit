Master's thesis · starting kit

Where should the robot stop?

You will build a working automation for a small business, run it for a month, and write up what happened. The workflows are already here. The thinking is the part that has to be yours.

01

The idea, in one minute

Almost every small business runs the same five steps. Someone gets in touch, you arrange to look at the job, you send a price, you chase it up, and eventually you invoice.

The automation runs all five steps. It is not allowed to send a price on its own, though. It drafts one, and a person checks it before it goes out.

That rule is the thesis. Anyone can build an automation and say so. The part worth a master’s is the argument underneath it: you let the computer handle four of these steps and not the fifth, and you can show why.

Let it decide

Much the same every time, and cheap to undo when it goes wrong. Sorting incoming messages. Suggesting appointment times. Sending a reminder.

Keep a person

Different every time, and expensive when it goes wrong. Pricing the work. Anything a customer cannot un-see once it has left your outbox.

03

What you actually do

  1. Find a small business that will let you watch. Three to eight people is about right. This is the hardest step and everything else depends on it, so start now.
  2. Watch how they work now, before changing anything. Follow five or six jobs from first message to getting paid, and write down how long each step takes. Boring, and completely essential. Without it you have nothing to compare against at the end.
  3. Draw your own line. For each step, two questions: is it different every time, and how bad is it if it goes wrong? Mostly the same and harmless when wrong, let the computer do it. Different every time and expensive when wrong, keep a person on it. Your answers may not match mine, and where they differ is worth writing about.
  4. Import the workflows and get them running. The files are ready to load. You will change things to fit your business, and those changes are evidence, so keep a note of them.
  5. Run it for a month and measure. Above all: when the computer drafts a price, what does the owner change before sending it?
  6. Write it up. What you built, why you drew the line where you did, and what actually happened.

One thing to watch for while it runs. If the owner stops correcting one part of the drafted price after a few weeks but carries on correcting another part, that is your result. It shows where the computer earned trust and where it did not, and you will have the numbers rather than an opinion.

Worth knowing

These workflows have been checked carefully, but nobody has run them on a live system yet. Expect to fix a couple of small things the first time you import one. That is normal. Write down what you had to fix, because it is useful material later.
