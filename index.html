00

The brief, and what this is

Your supervisor asked for a live workflow automation built on n8n, wrapped so that someone other than you can use it, with the thesis itself being the documentation and justification of that build. The wrapper is not decoration — it is the productisation claim, and it will be graded.

This page is a scaffold: industry, architecture, method and evaluation protocol specified so you can start on Monday rather than spending three weeks deciding what to build. You build it and you write it. That is the point of the brief and it is your name on the submission — what a scaffold can give you is the scoping and review that is hard to get at this level of detail.

There is no client material here. The reference business described in §02 is a composite of publicly observable characteristics of small trade contractors, not any real engagement. That matters for you as well as for anyone else: a thesis you can publish, show in interviews and defend without confidentiality caveats is worth more than one you cannot.

The one decision to make first

Pick your reference business and get access to it. Everything downstream depends on it. In descending order of thesis quality:

  1. A real trade business that lets you observe and instrument it. Real baseline, real results chapter. One owner-operator doing 20–60 jobs a month is enough. Worth two weeks of cold outreach.
  2. A real business you can observe but not instrument. Genuine process map, genuine observed estimates; your results become a simulation validated against real inputs. Still strong, stated honestly.
  3. A fully constructed reference case. Weakest, but viable — provided you are explicit that the contribution is the design and the method, not a measured effect.
Hold the line

Do not slide from option 1 to option 3 silently halfway through. Whichever you are on when you write up, the methods chapter says so plainly. Examiners forgive a modest design; they do not forgive one that was quietly downgraded.

01

Method: from observed process to justified boundary

Five stages, each producing an artefact the next one consumes. The value is traceability — every element of the final build points back to an observation rather than to an opinion.

  1. Discovery by subject-following

    Do not map the org chart. Map the job: pick the unit of work the business sells — one quote, one installation, one service call — and follow single instances end to end, from first contact to cash received.

    • Observe 5–8 complete instances. Fewer is anecdote; more is past the point of new information at this scale.
    • Record each step as actor, trigger, tool touched, output, wait time, rework flag.
    • Record the exception path separately. That is usually where the cost lives, and it is almost always missing from how an owner describes their own process.
    • Ask the owner to describe the process before you observe it, and keep that description. The gap between described and observed process is a finding in its own right, and a publishable one.

    Output — a BPMN process model. Use the standard so your examiner is evaluating your analysis, not your notation. One diagram per happy path, exception branches annotated.

  2. Quantify what it costs today

    For each step: time per instance × instances per period × loaded hourly rate. Hold to these rules, because this is where theses lose credibility:

    • Measured, estimated and assumed are three different labels. A stopwatch reading is measured. "About twenty minutes" is estimated. A loaded rate built from an award wage plus on-costs is assumed — and you state the assumption. Label every figure, in every table.
    • Never present an estimate as a measurement. Not in text, not in a chart.
    • Wait time is not labour cost, but it is not nothing. Separate column, discussed as a lead-time effect.
    • Include rework explicitly. A step redone 30% of the time costs 1.3×.

    Output — a costed process table. This is the baseline your results chapter compares against.

  3. Place the automation boundary

    The core intellectual move, and the part most student projects skip by automating whatever is technically easiest. Score every step on three axes:

    Scoring axes
    AxisLowHigh
    Variance
    how much do instances differ?    		Same every timeEvery one is a judgement call
    Consequence of error
    what does a wrong output cost?    		Trivially correctedMoney, safety or reputation
    Data availability
    is the input structured and reachable?    		Sitting in an APIIn someone's head

    Then map scores to decisions:

    • Low variance, low consequence, high availability → fully automate.
    • High variance, low consequence → draft-and-review. The system proposes, the human disposes.
    • Any high consequence → human decides; automation only assembles the evidence.
    • Low data availability → do not automate yet. The prior project is capturing the data. Saying so in a thesis is a finding, not a failure.

    Output — an annotated model where every step carries a boundary decision and the three scores justifying it. Your examiner can now disagree with a score rather than with your whole design, which is exactly where you want to be at a defence.

  4. Build

    Detailed in §02. Two points are methodological and belong here:

    • Build the instrumentation before the automation. Logging added afterwards means no baseline and no results chapter. This is the single most common way these projects fail.
    • Version prompts and workflows. Export n8n workflow JSON into git at every meaningful change. A system containing an LLM whose prompts were edited in a web UI and never recorded is not reproducible — and reproducibility is a grading criterion.

  5. Measure

    Detailed in §03. Designed before you build, not after.

What makes this a thesis rather than a project write-up

The contribution is not "I built an automation." It is a defensible answer to a question:

In a high-variance, low-data-maturity small business, where should the boundary between automated and human decision sit — and what evidence justifies placing it there?

Everything above is the apparatus for answering it. State the question in the introduction; answer it in the conclusion with your own boundary decisions as the evidence. If a boundary you placed turned out to be wrong during the build, that is your most valuable single result. Write it up rather than hiding it.

02

The system to build

Reference business

An owner-operated trade contractor — electrical, HVAC, landscaping or joinery — with 3–12 staff, 20–80 jobs a month, quotes ranging from a few hundred to a few tens of thousands. The characteristics that drive the design:

  • The owner is also the estimator, so quoting competes with billable work and happens at night.
  • Enquiries arrive by phone, web form, email and text, and are not in one place.
  • Quote-to-decision lag is long and follow-up is inconsistent. Revenue leaks here — not in win rate on quotes actually chased.
  • Invoicing lags job completion because whoever finished the job is not whoever invoices.
  • The existing stack is a calendar, a spreadsheet, an accounting package, and memory.

Why five and not one

Two reasons, and both belong in the thesis. Individually each workflow is unremarkable; connected, they change the unit of work. The job record persists across all five and accumulates state — that is the difference between task automation and process automation, and it is a claim you can defend.

And it lets you show a compounding lead-time effect. Each workflow removes a wait, and the interesting result is total quote-to-cash elapsed time, which no single workflow could move. Report both the per-step labour saving and the end-to-end lead time; they tell different stories, and the second is the better one.

The Wippli layer, read properly

"Productisation" is the part most students under-read. Treat it as three separate claims:

  1. Input form — the intake surface, so the system does not depend on the owner configuring n8n. Non-trivial: the form's field design determines W1's extraction quality, which makes form design an engineering decision rather than decoration.
  2. Output visualisation — the review queue where the owner approves drafted quotes and variations. This is where your human-in-the-loop boundary becomes a literal screen. Screenshot it; it is the clearest possible evidence of the design argument.
  3. Transferability — the claim that the thing moves to a second business without a rebuild. Test it rather than asserting it: what is configuration (rate card, service area, crew, thresholds) and what is code? If a second business needs workflow surgery, say so. A negative finding on transferability is a real result and examiners reward it.

03

Measurement design

Design this before you build. Build first and you will have no baseline, and the results chapter degrades into a description of features.

Baseline

Four weeks minimum of the process running unchanged, instrumented. If you cannot instrument it, timestamp what already exists — enquiry emails, calendar entries, quote PDFs, invoice dates in the accounting package. Most of the lead-time series can be reconstructed from artefacts nobody thought of as data.

Capture per job instance: enquiry received → qualified; qualified → site visit; site visit → quote sent; quote sent → decision; job complete → invoice issued; invoice issued → paid. Plus owner time per stage (self-reported diary, labelled estimated), number of follow-up touches, and outcome — won, lost, or no decision.

Twenty to thirty instances gives you something to talk about. It does not give you statistical power, and you say so.

Outcomes

Primary: end-to-end quote-to-cash elapsed time. One number, moved by the system as a whole, robust to noise in any single step. This is your headline.

Secondary: owner hours per job on administrative steps; quote turnaround from site visit to quote sent (likely your largest single effect); follow-up touches actually delivered versus intended; invoice lag; and win rate — reported with the confound flagged.

Instrumentation

Every workflow writes a structured event to the job record: workflow, node, in, out, duration, error, human-intervened. Build this as a reusable sub-workflow called from all five, on day one.

For W3, additionally capture the drafted quote and the approved quote, both stored; edit distance between them per line item; review duration from queue-open to approve; and which fields the owner overrode.

Your best chart

Override rate by field, plotted over weeks. It tells you where model judgement is trusted and where it is not, and it shows whether the boundary should move as the owner calibrates. If you produce one memorable exhibit in the whole thesis, make it this one — it answers the research question directly, with data nobody else in your cohort will have.

Confounds you must handle

  • Hawthorne. The owner knows they are being measured and will chase quotes harder. Declare it, and lean on metrics they cannot easily improve by trying — system-timestamped lags — over ones they can, like touches delivered.
  • Seasonality. Trades are seasonal and weather-dependent. Four weeks of baseline against four weeks of treatment in different months is not a clean comparison. Report job mix in both windows and say so.
  • Selection. If the system runs only on new enquiries while complex jobs stay manual, your sample is the easy half. Either route everything through it or report the split.
  • Market conditions contaminate win rate and you cannot control for them at n=25. Report it, attribute nothing, and explain why.

Honesty rules

These are not ethics boilerplate. They are what protects you at a defence.

  • n is small. It is a case study, not an experiment. Frame it as one in the methods chapter and no examiner can attack it as a failed experiment.
  • Distinguish measured, estimated and assumed on every figure, in every table.
  • Report what the system got wrong. A results chapter with only wins reads as advocacy — and an examiner who finds a failure you did not report has found your weakest moment.
  • If an effect sits inside the noise, say it sits inside the noise.
  • Label extrapolation as extrapolation, and keep it out of the abstract entirely.

The qualitative strand

Numbers from one business will not carry the thesis alone. Add a structured interview with the owner before and after — the same questions both times, so you can quote the shift. And keep your own build log: what you expected, what broke, what you changed and why. That is primary data for the discussion chapter and it is free if you keep it as you go. Write it weekly; it cannot be reconstructed at the end.

Where to stop

Scope creep kills these projects. Out of scope — and say so in the limitations section rather than building them: a mobile app for field crew; two-way accounting sync (read-only is enough); payment processing; anything for a second business beyond assessing transferability; rebuilding the CRM.

Four workflows working and measured beats six workflows half-built. If week 9 is tight, cut W5 and explain the cut.

Failure modes, and what each one actually means

  • No business by week 3. Switch to observe-only or a constructed reference and say so in methods. Do not keep waiting into week 6.
  • No baseline captured. Fatal to the results chapter. If week 4 arrives without one, stop building and fix it.
  • W3 quality is poor. A finding, not a failure. Report where model judgement was insufficient and what data would have been needed — a better contribution than a working quote generator.
  • The owner stops using it. The most informative outcome in the whole project. Interview immediately, while the reason is fresh. Adoption failure in SME automation is a genuine research contribution, under-reported precisely because students bury it.

What to send Roger, and when

  • End of week 4 — process model and boundary decisions. Confirms the framing before you build on it.
  • End of week 8 — the W3 review interface working. Confirms the productisation element he asked for.
  • End of week 11 — full draft.
05

Theory anchors

You need scaffolding for the literature chapter. These are the standard hooks — your library will surface better recent work, and you should let it.

  • BPM lifecycle — Dumas et al., Fundamentals of Business Process Management. Gives you the discovery / analysis / redesign / implementation / monitoring frame this whole method sits inside.
  • RPA task-selection criteria. Your variance / consequence / availability scoring is a contribution against this baseline, so read enough of it to say precisely what yours adds.
  • Levels of automation and human-in-the-loop — Parasuraman & Sheridan is the classic citation. This is where your core question actually lives, and it is the literature your examiner will expect you to have found.
  • Technology adoption in SMEs — for the discussion chapter, on why the binding constraint is so rarely the technology.
